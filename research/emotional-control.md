# Top-down emotional control — research notes and a design

Notes towards a companion to `apps/more/hallucination/`: a trainer that does
for feeling what Controlled Hallucination does for seeing. It covers the
model the design rests on, what the evidence says can and cannot be
controlled, the niche practices that have pushed furthest, and a concrete
exercise ladder that mirrors the visual one.

## The model: emotion as a controlled hallucination of the body

The visual trainer rests on one idea: seeing is the brain's best guess,
built top-down and corrected bottom-up. Emotion research has converged on
the same shape.

- **Constructed emotion.** Lisa Feldman Barrett's account (*How Emotions Are
  Made*, 2017) treats an emotion as the brain's prediction of what its
  bodily signals mean in context. A racing heart is fear on a cliff edge,
  excitement before a race, attraction on a date. The signal is bottom-up;
  which emotion it becomes is decided top-down.
- **Interoceptive inference.** Anil Seth and Hugo Critchley frame this as
  perception of the body's interior: the same predictive machinery as
  vision, pointed inwards. Seth calls ordinary perception a "controlled
  hallucination"; on this view a feeling is one too.
- **Two factors.** Older and blunter: Schachter and Singer (1962) injected
  adrenaline and found people's feelings followed the situation they were
  put in. Dutton and Aron's suspension-bridge study (1974) found men on a
  swaying bridge rated a woman they met there as more attractive, borrowing
  arousal from the fear.

So the two levers are the same as in vision: **loosen the bottom-up signal**
(change the body, or leave it ambiguous) and **strengthen the top-down
reading** (attention, appraisal, imagery, concepts).

## What "complete control" can and cannot mean

The honest answer matters for the design.

- **Control of the reading is large; control of the signal is partial.**
  Reappraisal reliably changes how strong a feeling is. Arousal itself
  moves more slowly, and mostly through the body: breathing, posture,
  muscle tension.
- **Suppression backfires.** Hiding an expression or pushing a feeling away
  tends to keep the physiology high and costs memory and attention (James
  Gross's work on suppression versus reappraisal). Thought suppression has
  a rebound: told not to think of a white bear, people think of it more
  (Daniel Wegner, 1987). A trainer aiming at "control" has to train
  changing the reading, not clamping the output.
- **The experts look regulated, not empty.** Decorated bomb-disposal
  operators had *lower* heart rates than equally skilled, undecorated
  colleagues under a lab stressor — yet reported the same bodily
  sensations (Stanley Rachman's courage studies). Alex Honnold's amygdala
  barely responded to images that lit up a control's, and the
  neuroscientist who scanned him, Jane Joseph, read it as tight regulation
  in someone very high in sensation-seeking, not as absent fear. Control
  shows up as a body that stays quieter while the feeling is still known.
- **Morita therapy**, a Japanese approach from the 1920s, makes the
  counterpoint explicitly: feelings are like weather, not directly
  controllable, and the work is acting well while they pass
  (*arugamama*, "as it is"). Worth keeping on the page as the limit.

## Niche practices that went furthest

Each of these is a worked-out method for top-down control of feeling or
body, and each maps onto an exercise below.

- **Tummo, Tibetan "inner fire".** Nuns in eastern Tibet raised their core
  temperature into the low-fever range (up to 38.3 °C) during the forceful
  breath form of g-tummo, and the traditional demonstration is drying wet
  sheets on the body in sub-zero air. Maria Kozhevnikov's 2013 study split
  the method in two: the *vase breath* produces the heat; the
  *visualisation* of flames along the spine helps keep it. Imagery working
  alongside a bodily lever, not instead of one.
  [Kozhevnikov et al. 2013](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/236106426_Neurocognitive_and_Somatic_Components_of_Temperature_Increases_during_g-Tummo_Meditation_Legend_and_Reality)
- **Open presence and the startle reflex.** Matthieu Ricard, a monk with
  about forty years of practice, was hit with a 115 dB burst of noise while
  meditating. In "open presence" his startle — face and body — was smaller
  than in focused meditation or in controls. The startle is about as
  bottom-up as a response gets, and a mental stance changed its size.
  [Levenson, Ekman & Ricard 2012](https://www.matthieuricard.org/en/articles/meditation-and-the-startle-response-a-case-study-by-robert-w-levenson-paul-ekman-and-matthieu-ricard-emotion-2012-12-3/)
- **The directed facial action task.** Ekman, Levenson and Friesen (1983)
  coached twelve actors and four face researchers, muscle by muscle, into
  the configurations of anger, fear, sadness and so on, without naming the
  emotion. Heart rate and finger temperature shifted with the pose: faster
  for anger, fear and sadness, warmer fingers for anger. Reliving a memory
  produced similar changes. The body can be moved from the outside in.
  [Ekman, Levenson & Friesen 1983](https://bpl.studentorg.berkeley.edu/docs/25-Autonomic%20nervous%20system83.pdf)
- **Stanislavski's emotion memory, Strasberg's sense memory.** Method
  actors do not try to feel an emotion directly. They rebuild the sensory
  details of a past moment — the cup's heat, the steam, the taste — until
  the feeling comes with them. Strasberg's training starts with the
  "morning drink": hold the cup, then rebuild it in pantomime with nothing
  in your hands. A ladder from bottom-up (a real cup) to top-down (an
  imagined one), the same as the visual trainer's.
  [Affective memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affective_memory) ·
  [Strasberg's sense memory](https://actorstoolkit.co.uk/sense-memory/)
- **Metta, loving-kindness, in its classical order.** The *Visuddhimagga*
  has practitioners generate goodwill towards themselves first, then a
  close friend, a neutral person, a difficult person, and finally everyone.
  An ordering by how much the target resists the feeling — the emotional
  version of "the screen supplies less and less".
- **Tonglen.** Tibetan "sending and taking": breathe in someone's distress,
  breathe out relief. A deliberate inversion of the usual reaction, trained
  until it holds.
- **Kasina, again.** The visual trainer's eyes-closed afterimage comes from
  the same manuals as metta, and the stages carry over: a copy of the
  stimulus that fades, then a version that is yours. In emotional terms:
  the afterglow of a real experience, then the same feeling without it.
- **Affect labelling.** Putting a feeling into words damps the amygdala's
  response to it (Matthew Lieberman and colleagues, 2007). Barrett's
  "emotional granularity" extends this: people with finer emotion words —
  *wistful* rather than *bad* — regulate better. This is the emotional
  counterpart of the visual trainer's ambiguity tiers, from Loose to Exact.
- **Self-distancing.** Talking to yourself in the third person, by name,
  lowers distress under social stress (Ethan Kross and colleagues, 2014).
  The Stoics' "view from above" — seeing the scene from a great height — is
  the imagery version.
- **Reappraising arousal as excitement.** Told to say "I am excited" rather
  than "calm down" before singing or speaking in public, people performed
  better (Alison Wood Brooks, 2014). The arousal stays; its reading changes.
- **Hypnotic pain modulation.** Pierre Rainville's 1997 study changed how
  *unpleasant* pain felt, separately from how *intense* it felt, by
  suggestion alone, and the change tracked activity in the anterior
  cingulate. The affective layer of even a raw sensation is open to
  top-down control.
- **Box breathing and resonance breathing.** Breathing at about six breaths
  a minute raises heart-rate variability (Paul Lehrer's resonance
  frequency work). Military "tactical breathing" (four counts in, hold,
  out, hold) is the field version. The most reliable bottom-up lever there
  is, and the one a phone camera can actually measure.

## The ladder: an exercise per rung

Ordered, as in the visual trainer, from "the world supplies the feeling" to
"you supply it". Each rung names its visual counterpart.

| # | Exercise | The world supplies | You control | Visual counterpart |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Breath** | a bodily lever | arousal, through the breath | — (the body channel vision lacks) |
| 2 | **Afterglow** | a real feeling, briefly (a piece of music, a photo) | keeping it after the source stops | Afterimage, Eyes closed |
| 3 | **Gaps** | music that swells, then cuts to silence, the silences lengthening | holding the feeling through the silences | Gaps |
| 4 | **Two readings** | an ambiguous face on a continuum from one expression to another | which way you read it | Two readings |
| 5 | **Reframe** | a mildly unpleasant image, twice | how strong it feels, by reappraising | Noise, seeded |
| 6 | **Distance** | a mildly upsetting memory you choose | moving it away, shrinking it, seeing it from outside | Steer it |
| 7 | **Sense memory** | nothing but an instruction | rebuilding a moment's sensations until the feeling comes | Generate |
| 8 | **Open sitting** | nothing at all | noticing feelings arise, without grabbing | Ganzfeld |

Notes on each:

1. **Breath.** Paced breathing at about six a minute, led by an expanding
   and contracting shape. The phone camera can read pulse from a fingertip
   over the lens, so heart rate and its variability can be shown. This is
   the one rung with a direct physiological measure.
2. **Afterglow.** Twenty seconds of a chosen piece of music or a chosen
   photograph, then silence and a grey screen; keep the feeling. Rated
   like an afterimage. Savouring research (Fred Bryant) is the closest
   literature.
3. **Gaps.** The emotional version of the visual exercise: the source comes
   and goes, each return weaker, each gap longer. The source has to be
   the user's own, since no stock clip moves everyone.
4. **Two readings.** Faces morphed from, say, fearful to surprised. On Watch
   trials the user just categorises; on Hold trials they try to read every
   face one way. The shift in the point where they switch category is an
   **objective measure of top-down control**, the same role the dominance
   ratio plays for the Necker cube. Needs a licensed face set (the Radboud
   or KDEF sets are available for research use) or synthetic faces.
5. **Reframe.** Each image is seen once on Watch and once on Reframe; the
   user rates intensity each time. The gap between the two is the control
   score. The OASIS image set is openly licensed and normed for
   unpleasantness. Mild images only.
6. **Distance.** Uses the visual skills directly: recall the memory, then
   push the scene away, drain its colour, see yourself in it from outside.
   This is where the hallucination trainer becomes groundwork.
7. **Sense memory.** Strasberg's progression: first a real object (a warm
   cup), then the same object rebuilt from memory with empty hands, then a
   whole moment rebuilt until its feeling arrives. The target feeling gets
   more specific as the level rises — *warm* before *tender*, *tender*
   before *the moment my brother came home* — which is the ambiguity
   tightening that the visual trainer does with symbols.
8. **Open sitting.** Ganzfeld for feeling: sit for several minutes, label
   each feeling as it arises in one word, as precisely as possible. The
   labels are logged; granularity (how varied and specific they are) is a
   measure in its own right.

## Measures, strongest first

1. **Psychometric shift** on ambiguous faces (Two readings): objective.
2. **Heart rate and variability** from the camera (Breath, and as a check
   on any rung): physiological, noisy but real.
3. **Watch-versus-reframe gap** in intensity ratings: self-reported, but
   within-person, so useful.
4. **Granularity** of labels in Open sitting: behavioural.
5. **Self-rated holds** for Afterglow, Gaps, Distance and Sense memory: the
   weakest, as in the visual trainer.

## Safety, which this one needs more than the visual trainer

- **Unpleasant feelings are invited, never imposed.** Mild material only,
  opt-in for every negative rung, a stop button always visible, and the
  session ends on a neutral or pleasant rung.
- **Memories are the user's choice**, and the page says plainly not to use
  traumatic ones. Distancing a fresh grief or a trauma memory without
  support can make it worse.
- **It is not therapy**, and says so, with a pointer to proper help for
  anyone whose feelings are overwhelming rather than interesting.
- **Breath work** stays at slow, comfortable rates. No forceful or
  hyperventilating breathing (the tummo forceful breath included) on the
  page: it can cause fainting.
